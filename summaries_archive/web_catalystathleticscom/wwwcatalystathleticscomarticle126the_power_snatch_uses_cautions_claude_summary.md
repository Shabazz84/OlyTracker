# Analysis: Power Snatch Article by Greg Everett

## Relevant to This Athlete's Profile

**1. Programming Principles**
- Power snatch as **reduced intensity substitute** for full snatch (preserves skill while lowering leg fatigue)—useful given athlete's chronic back pain
- Power snatch in **complexes** (power snatch + snatch) to improve technical carryover
- Variety tool to prevent burnout during hypertrophy block

**2. Technique Cues**
- **Power snatch ≠ different mechanics from snatch**—should receive identically; no hesitation at bar receipt
- Avoid widening feet excessively in power position (increases injury risk on miss, stresses hips/knees)
- Keep arms relaxed; don't anticipate getting bar high (prevents tension, maintains turnover speed)
- Receive "relatively high" and ride down into squat (not stopping abruptly at parallel)

**3. Exercise Recommendations**
- **Power snatch as teaching tool**: Given athlete's weak OHS (50 kg), power snatch bypasses mobility demand while teaching aggression, extension, and turnover
- **Power snatch + snatch complexes**: Direct application for this athlete—power snatch teaches high bar receipt, then snatch with same weight enforces meeting bar at same height before descending
- Best for: technical work, warm-ups (if done correctly), or intensity reduction

**4. Recovery/Mobility**
- Power snatch useful for **flexibility-limited athletes** (athlete has mobility-limited OHS)—defers heavy mobility work while building snatch pattern
- Reduces leg fatigue vs. full snatch

**5. Cautions for This Athlete**
- Risk: Athlete may develop hesitation or bad habits if power snatch becomes primary; **must pair with full snatches + OHS work** to prevent mechanical drift
- Chronic back pain: Avoid forward hip hinge compensation (mentioned as common fault)
