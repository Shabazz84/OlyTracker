# https://www.catalystathletics.com/article/2194/Split-Jerk-Power-Jerk-Squat-Jerk-Why-Who/

**1. Programming Principles**
*   **Frequency/Periodization:** The text advocates for becoming "moderately competent" in all three jerk styles (Split, Power, Squat) as training exercises before settling on a primary competitive style. It suggests spending the bulk of effort developing proficiency in one chosen style over time.
*   **Intensity/Sets/Reps:** No specific numbers provided; emphasizes that the power jerk is a "comparatively rare competitive style" but has high utility for training, particularly when the athlete can drive the bar very high and maintain balance.

**2. Technique Cues**
*   **Dip & Drive (Universal):** Straight down, straight up, with the bar moving slightly backward into the overhead position. Do not intentionally limit elevation to get lower; aim to elevate the bar first, then drop under it as weight increases.
*   **Split Jerk Stability:** Use a broad base (width at least that of a squat) for stability in all directions. Allow for corrective adjustments by shifting forward/backward or side-to-side *after* receiving the bar to stabilize overhead.
*   **Recovery Mechanics:** In a split, recover by "wedging up" under the bar through alternating small inward movements of each foot rather than driving straight up from a dead stop.

**3. Exercise Recommendations**
*   **Primary Focus:** Split Jerk (recommended for new lifters and most competitive contexts due to stability and depth).
*   **Secondary/Training:** Power Jerk (Push Jerk) – useful for athletes with high bar elevation ability or as a training tool even if the split is the primary style.
*   **Tertiary:** Squat Jerk – generally not recommended unless the athlete possesses exceptional mobility, leg strength, and precision; primarily used by lifters who cannot elevate the bar well but have great mobility.

**4. Recovery or Mobility Advice**
*   **Mobility Constraints:** The Power/Squat jerk requires significant overhead mobility to maintain a vertical trunk at depth. If an athlete is immobile, they should not force a low receiving position in these styles as it creates huge demand on shoulder/upper back mobility and forward torso inclination.
*   **Knee Mechanics:** Avoid deep parallel positions in the Power Jerk if knee leverage is poor; the split allows for greater hip depth with better knee mechanics (similar to a parallel squat) without the same leverage disadvantages.

**5. Relevance to Athlete Profile**
*   **Jerk Style Selection:** The text strongly supports the athlete's current use of the **Push/Power Jerk** as a valid training tool, noting it is easier for those who can drive high and have good balance. However, it warns that the Power Jerk has "little margin for error" in bar position compared to the Split.
*   **Chronic Back Pain:** The text highlights that the Split Jerk allows for a more vertical trunk position (less forward inclination) than the Power/Squat jerk at similar depths, which may reduce shear forces on the lower back if mobility is limited.
*   **OHS Limitation (50kg):** The article notes that the Squat Jerk is often impossible due to "narrow-grip overhead squat" mobility limits. Since 50kg OHS is a primary limiter, attempting a Squat Jerk style for competition would likely be counterproductive unless mobility improves significantly.
*   **Transition Advice:** As an intermediate athlete transitioning to Olympic lifting, the text advises learning and practicing the Split Jerk until there is a "good reason" to switch, rather than abandoning it early due to technical difficulty.
