# Train less, lift more! Weightlifting

## Key Points
*   **Injury-Driven Periodization:** Progression must be paused or modified during back spasms ("locking up"). Heavy loading should only proceed when the body is stable; otherwise, switch to lighter pulls or technique work.
*   **Weak Link Compensation:** Chronic back pain often stems from weak glutes and abs failing to stabilize heavy loads in flexion (snatch/clean), causing the spine to seize. Hypertrophy blocks must prioritize these stabilizers over maximal lifting.
*   **Non-Linear Progression:** Plateaus are protective mechanisms against injury, not just lack of effort. Long-term consistency (months) is required before breaking through stagnation; "storming" max attempts is dangerous for athletes with a history of back issues.
*   **Mobility vs. Hypertrophy Balance:** Excessive development in forearms/traps can restrict shoulder rotation and turnover. A hypertrophy block should target functional posterior chain muscles (core, glutes) while avoiding isolation work that tightens the grip or limits range of motion.
*   **Recovery Management:** Heavy sessions (e.g., 225kg squats) require significant recovery time to resolve stiffness. Training through back spasms is contraindicated; listen to the body and finish early if fatigue compromises form.

## Technique Cues
*   **Setup Integrity:** Maintain a rigid bar path; do not let the bar "collapse" or break position during the pull, which increases shear force on the lower back.
*   **Grip & Turnover:** Avoid excessive forearm hypertrophy ("too pumped") which restricts wrist mobility and shoulder rotation. Focus on wrist flexion/extension exercises to ensure smooth turnover in the clean/snatch.
*   **Jerk Stability (Push Jerk):** Keep shoulders stable during the drive; avoid "breaking" elbows or allowing them to move away from the bar. Since you use a push jerk, prioritize wrist stability over split-jerk footwork mechanics.
*   **Breathing & Post-Lift:** Do not stand too long under the bar after a lift; breathe immediately to prevent being "choked out" by fatigue and reduce spinal compression time.

## Programming Principles
*   **Hypertrophy Block Focus (6 Weeks):** Prioritize **Abdominal work + Hyperextensions** as mandatory core stability exercises. This addresses the root cause of chronic back pain before increasing squat volume.
*   **Accessory Selection:** Use machines or isolation lifts to target weak links (glutes, abs) if classic lifts induce pain. Avoid heavy bicep/forearm curls that increase grip stiffness.
*   **Load Management:** Increase weight in small increments (1–3kg) rather than large jumps once the body adapts. Focus on sub-maximal volume (e.g., 85% intensity) to build strength without risking failure or injury.
*   **Frequency/Intensity:** Adopt a low-frequency, high-intensity approach for heavy days with adequate rest between sessions. Reduce warm-up sets as proficiency increases to conserve energy for working weights.
*   **Adaptability:** If the athlete feels mentally or physically unable to perform (common in night shift workers), move the session or finish early rather than forcing a suboptimal lift.

## Athlete Relevance
*   **Chronic Back Pain:** The plan explicitly addresses your pain by mandating core/glute hypertrophy before heavy squats/cleans. It validates stopping heavy loading during spasms and switching to pulls, aligning with the "protective mechanism" theory of stalling.
*   **Push Jerk Limitation:** While the text discusses split jerk mechanics generally, it reinforces that wrist stability is critical for the clean/snatch phase regardless of the jerk style. The advice to reduce forearm bulk directly benefits your ability to turnover without shoulder restriction.
*   **Night Shift Schedule:** The emphasis on "conserving energy," avoiding unnecessary fatigue, and trusting the process during stagnation supports an athlete with irregular sleep patterns who cannot afford burnout or injury spikes.
*   **OHS Limitation (50kg):** The focus on wrist mobility and forearm flexibility addresses the likely bottleneck in your overhead stability, as tight forearms often restrict the necessary shoulder extension for a strict press/snatch.
