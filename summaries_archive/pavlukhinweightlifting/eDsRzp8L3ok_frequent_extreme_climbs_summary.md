# Frequent extreme climbs

## Key Points
*   **Stress vs. Adaptation:** Heavy lifting induces hormonal and nervous system exhaustion even without muscle soreness; recovery requires 48+ hours for hormonal balance. Training must distinguish between "training records" (max effort) and "competition records" (successful lifts).
*   **Injury Mitigation via Volume Management:** High-rep heavy squats (e.g., 5x5, 10 reps) create prolonged static loading time, increasing injury risk for athletes with chronic back pain. The preferred method is single repetitions at high intensity to recruit fibers while minimizing joint compression duration.
*   **Failure Protocol:** Attempts where the athlete feels they will bail out are "empty work." The coach mandates completing the lift (even if failing at the top) provided technical intent was maintained, rather than dropping early.
*   **Recovery Requirements:** Full CNS recovery between heavy attempts requires ~5 minutes of rest. Insufficient caloric intake necessitates supplementation to support protein synthesis and nervous system function.

## Technique Cues
*   **Chest Integrity:** "Hold the chest up." A collapsed chest ("breaks") compromises the lift; this is critical for athletes with upper back weakness who tend to lean forward.
*   **Explosive Extension:** The extension of hips and knees at the bottom must be rapid. Slow extensions (>3 seconds) indicate a lack of posterior chain explosiveness.
*   **Overcoming Inertia:** Focus on the "dead point" where the bar stops; maximum effort is required here to overcome inertia.
*   **Jerk Mechanics (Drop Under):** The primary error is pushing rather than dropping under. Once the bar reaches forehead level, the athlete must drop under it and press through the legs. The intent should be to "get rid of the weight," not just push it up.
    *   *Note:* While the source mentions scissors/split mechanics, the principle of dropping under applies to the Push Jerk constraint as well.

## Programming Principles
*   **High Frequency, Low Volume for Heavy Loads:** Instead of traditional block periodization with high volume, utilize a "heavy week" structure where heavy compounds (e.g., Squats) are performed frequently but with low rep counts (singles).
*   **Progression via Attempting:** Drive adaptation by attempting weights slightly above current maxes (e.g., attempting 136kg when the working max is ~130kg), provided technical integrity is maintained.
*   **Phase Separation:** The source focuses on heavy strength and technique; it does not prescribe hypertrophy blocks. For a dedicated 6-week hypertrophy block, this philosophy must be adapted by using moderate weights with higher volume, or strictly separating the two phases as planned.

## Athlete Relevance
*   **Chronic Back Pain:** The "single rep" heavy lifting strategy is highly relevant; it reduces spinal compression time compared to high-rep sets, potentially mitigating flare-ups while maintaining mechanical tension.
*   **Night Shift Schedule:** The emphasis on managing CNS fatigue and ensuring adequate nutrition/supplementation is critical for a night shift worker (Wed–Sun) where sleep cycles are disrupted. Recovery windows must be strictly respected to prevent "stress that destroys" adaptation.
*   **Transition Phase:** The advice to attempt slightly heavier weights helps break plateaus but requires careful monitoring given the athlete's intermediate status and transition from powerlifting.
*   **Upper Back Weakness:** The observation of forward leaning aligns with the need for hypertrophy work on traps/upper back, which is a primary limiter in the OHS (50 kg) and snatch stability.
