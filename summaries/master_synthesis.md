# Olympic Weightlifting Coaching Synthesis

## Consensus Principles

**Technique quality is the foundation of long-term progress in the snatch.** Multiple sources emphasize that perfect (or near-perfect) technique is what produces the best and most consistent results [1], that technical models are proven and following them leads to faster, more consistent progress [13], and that technique becomes "ingrained" only through diligent, correct practice over time [7].

**The split jerk is generally favored over the power/push jerk for maximizing weight moved, due to margin for error.** [31] states the split jerk offers "every possible great advantage" while the power jerk has a small margin for error and is unforgiving if the bar drifts forward. [37]/[56]/[59] (Catalyst Athletics) state the split jerk provides the greatest margin for error in bar placement overhead due to its broad base, least flexibility demand, and easiest recovery from imperfect positions, and is "a good choice for just about everyone," recommending new lifters not give up on it early [56]. [46] adds that squat jerks are missed far more often in competition than split jerks specifically because of this margin-for-error difference.

**Leg/lower-body strength underpins jerk (and squat) quality.** [35] states leg strength is a huge part of dip-and-drive speed and tempo in the split jerk. [52] similarly emphasizes front squats and strict pressing as key stability builders for the split jerk. [96]/[109] (squat topic) emphasize that longer-limbed/taller lifters need dedicated leg-strength accessory work to support good squat positions.

**Recovery/consistency is treated as an active, sleep-dependent variable, not automatic.** [146] frames recovery as a "line" that depends on sleep, mobility, and nutrition, and that training-recovery balance determines whether you regress or improve [153]. [148] states sleep is "the only thing" that fixes accumulated fatigue. [149] lists sleep as recovery priority number one. [155] states recovery (via sleep) is the thing many lifters struggle with most.

**Mobility work should be frequent/consistent rather than occasional.** [116] states more frequent mobility exposure improves and maintains capacity better than infrequent bouts. [118] recommends including overhead movement every day. [8]/[127] describe near-daily mobility/band-distraction work paired with soft tissue work as part of a lifter's routine.

**Coaching/technical feedback (an outside eye) is treated as highly valuable, even for experienced lifters.** [12] states a coach's feedback is valuable because "it's just impossible to be objective" about your own technique, even with extensive knowledge. [9] shows an athlete relying on periodic remote coaching (video review). [26] shows Greg Everett giving specific corrective exercises for self-reported technical errors, implying external diagnosis matters.

## Conflicts and How to Resolve Them

**Squat jerk vs. split jerk for female/younger athletes with high mobility but insufficient strength:** [133] notes a "paradox" where mobile athletes (often female/younger) are drawn to the squat jerk because they *can* do the mobility, but then fail because they lack the strength to lock out and brace overhead — suggesting mobility alone is not sufficient justification for choosing squat jerk. This doesn't directly contradict [56]'s general recommendation of split jerk for most, but adds nuance: the choice isn't purely about mobility capability. **Resolution:** For this document, treat [56] (split jerk as default recommendation for new lifters) as the primary guidance, with [133] as a caveat about why squat jerk temptation exists but may not translate to success.

**Behind-the-neck/push jerk as training tool vs. limited utility of pressing for jerk performance:** [42] recommends behind-the-neck jerks or push jerks for speed/power production in general athletes. Meanwhile [36] notes that some very strong jerkers (240kg+) had comparatively weak presses (90kg) and treated this as a "badge of honor," but the source pushes back, noting those same lifters have "incredibly inconsistent" jerks, implying pressing strength likely still matters for reliability — this is presented as the source's own counter-argument rather than a clean external conflict. **Resolution:** No true contradiction — present both: pressing power is discussed as beneficial [36], while behind-the-neck/push jerk variations are recommended as a general athletic training tool [42], not for competitive lift selection specifically.

**No explicit conflict was found on back health, periodization models, or general squat mechanics among sources — differences there are more about emphasis/context than direct disagreement.**
