### 1. Programming Principles
*   **Focus:** Aggressive leg drive (knee extension + ankle plantar flexion) during the pull phase of Snatch and Clean.
*   **Intensity/Frequency:** High-intensity pulls/high-pulls to reinforce force transfer; frequency should prioritize quality over volume given the athlete's chronic back pain.
*   **Periodization:** The text emphasizes a specific technical correction (leg drive) rather than a full periodization model, but implies this focus is foundational for all pull variations.

### 2. Technique Cues
*   **"Push harder with your legs":** Treat leg drive as the solid platform required for hip extension to be effective; without it, force is absorbed by "soft" legs (similar to jumping in sand).
*   **Vertical Trajectory:** Aggressive leg drive prevents hips from moving forward past the vertical plane, which pushes the bar horizontally.
*   **Balance:** Driving against the ground maintains balance over the foot; leaning forward/backward reduces the ability to drive forcefully.
*   **Harmony:** Hips provide speed; legs provide elevation. They must work in concert (extend hips aggressively while pushing feet against the ground with equal vigor).

### 3. Exercise Recommendations
*   **Pulls and High Pulls:** Specifically recommended by the author to increase leg drive.
*   *(Implicit)*: Snatch and Clean pulls focusing on knee extension timing.

### 5. Relevance to Athlete Profile
*   **Chronic Back Pain:** The text warns that failing to drive with legs forces hips to push the bar forward, often resulting in excessive back arching or leaning to compensate. Correct leg drive preserves balance and reduces shear force on the spine caused by poor hip-bar contact timing.
*   **Push Jerk Only (No Split):** While the article discusses Snatch/Clean pulls, the principle of "legs providing elevation" is critical for a push jerk athlete who lacks the split mechanics to recover; maximizing bar height via leg drive reduces the distance the arms must press.
*   **OHS Limiter:** The text states hips provide speed while legs provide elevation; improving leg drive directly addresses the need to get the bar higher, which may indirectly help an athlete struggling with overhead stability if they are currently compensating for a low pull.
