## Entry Gate

Block 1 ran on hang primaries. Block 2 moves the competition lifts to the floor.
The corpus supplies three checks worth running before week 9, and refuses to
supply a fourth.

**1. The hang ceiling.** Hang variations should sit at approximately **90% of
the lifts from the floor** [E9.5]. Once they reach that, they stop paying:
*"if we get to 90% of our hang snatch or 90% of our full snatch from the
floor... okay, that's definitely enough, it's not going to benefit us anymore,
we need to focus on that snatch and clean and jerk again"* [E9.7]. Block 1 built
hang numbers; that is the ratio that says the hang has done its job.

**2. The crutch check.** The same source warns that the high hang is *"generally
easier to hit big numbers with and can be a crutch for a lifter"*, producing *"a
psychological barrier to hitting PBs"* from the floor and emotional negativity
when the floor lift won't match [E9.5]. And lifters arrive at meets having done
*"loads of hangs or variations or complexes or blocks or pauses"* without the
floor repetitions they needed [E8.5]. Block 1's design carried that risk by
construction; week 9 is where it is paid off or not.

**3. The overhead check, unchanged from Block 1.** Snatch balance should reach
at least the best snatch, *"preferably... something like 15 kilos more or maybe
like 10 percent more than your best snatch pretty comfortably"* [E10.3, E10.1].
Until that clears, the snatch from the floor is limited by the receiving
position, not by the pull.

**What the corpus will not supply: a numeric gate.** Asked when a lifter moves
from power/hang work to the full lifts from the floor, the nearest answer is
that the timing is individual and can be fast: the top-down teaching method goes
*"overhead squat → high hang snatch or muscle snatch → and then we move
eventually to the floor. Sometimes that can be done in the same session,
sometimes it takes a month depending on that style of lifter"* [E31.4]. That is
a range, not a threshold. **Any specific pass/fail number for entering Block 2 is
[JUDGMENT — NO COVERAGE]** and none is prescribed here; the three checks above
are read together, by eye.

## The Floor Transition

This is what the block is for, and the corpus is unusually direct about it.

### The variation should never have been the whole program

*"We wouldn't ever really go through a program where we're doing no full
snatches from the floor in the weeks leading up and then as we get to the peak
we start to introduce them. We would always have the full lifts plus some
variation, then variation and complexity gets removed as you move towards the
peak"* [E31.4].

That is the architecture of Block 2 stated in one sentence, and it is also a
retrospective criticism of Block 1's hang-primary design. **Full lifts plus
variation from week 9; variation and complexity stripped progressively toward
week 16.**

### The exit protocol for the hang

Explicit, and it re-resolves to the same handle as in Block 1:
*"forget about the hang snatch — keep it in there for the next few weeks, just
keep it somewhere between three to four sets of two at **super lightweights**,
and let this [the full lift] take over"* [E8.8]. The same passage adds why it
stays at all: *"if it was working to support your technique along here then keep
it in there and let it support us"* [E8.8].

So the hang does not disappear at week 9. It demotes: 3–4 × 2, light, speed
preserved, as support — while the floor lift becomes the session's primary.

### Pull from the floor is harder than it looks, and that is the whole risk

Everett, describing his own teaching sequence: after the overhead squat and
snatch-balance series and the snatch from mid-thigh, *"we're going to learn the
starting position on the floor. We're going to learn how to pull from the floor
to that mid-thigh position — which is more nuanced and more technically
difficult than a lot of people assume, and they'll kind of just blow past it and
end up with all kinds of problems for years after"* [E10.10].

Block 2 is exactly the phase in which that gets blown past. The next three
subsections are the countermeasures the corpus actually names.

### Static start, not dynamic

*"If your pull from the floor feels weak or your position is inconsistent, the
first thing to do before you panic and completely change your training program is
to just make sure you're using a static start in all your deadlifts and pulls.
So, set that position tightly before you begin rather than using a dynamic start
of some kind... that dynamic start feels better because it creates some extra
force coming off the ground, which makes you have to use less basic strength.
That's also why it doesn't strengthen that initial pull as well. And it's also
creating the potential for inconsistent positions"* [E32.2].

The payoff is stated: training the static start *"is going to solidify that
position consistency and strengthen that very first part, which is then going to
make your body more naturally revert to that position when using a dynamic start
in a snatch or clean"* [E32.2].

**Prescription: static start on every pull and deadlift variation through
Block 2** [E32.2].

### First-pull positions

- **Shoulders stay in front of the bar** into the second-pull position: the first
  pull brings the lifter *"into the second pull position — weight balanced
  slightly more toward the heels, shoulders in front of the bar"*, and *"the
  shoulders shouldn't be behind the bar until partway through the second pull"*
  [E32.1]. Applies to both the snatch and the clean [E32.1].
- **Do not deliberately slow the first pull.** *"In 25 years of competitive
  weightlifting, I've never heard a coach of any significant experience tell an
  athlete to pull the bar slowly from the floor."* Where a new lifter's speed off
  the floor is causing back rounding or the hips shooting up, the fix is *"keep
  your back tighter"* or *"don't let your butt shoot straight up at the
  beginning"* — not "slow down" [E32.6].
- **Upright torso is taught, not waited for:** *"You get a lifter comfortable
  being more upright in the pull by coaching them to pull this way, technique
  work to teach it, and strength work to allow and reinforce it"* [E32.9].
- One cue at a time: *"you get one before the lift starts and one during the
  lift... It's far better to successfully correct one thing at a time than fail
  to correct five"* — and keep the same cues for a whole workout rather than
  changing them each set [E33.9].

### Second-pull timing — the fault the floor introduces

Going to the floor lengthens the runway before the extension, and the corpus
names the resulting error precisely.

- **Be patient.** *"A big mistake with the snatch and clean is trying to initiate
  the final explosion too soon. This can create a list of problems, including
  shifting your balance too far forward, pushing the bar away from your body,
  preventing a complete extension of the hips, and limiting the speed and height
  of the bar. Bring the barbell back into your body as it leaves the floor, and
  continue using the lats to push it back into the hips"* [E42.5].
- **Where to think about it:** *"keep the shoulders over the bar until upper
  thigh/hip... generally if you think of jumping when the bar is at the upper
  thigh in the clean and at the hip in the snatch, it will actually start a bit
  sooner, and this is what you want"* [E42.8].
- **Then pull under, on time, not early:** *"if you pull under fast enough and
  you actively try and get under faster... at the correct time, at the correct
  point in the lift, you're giving yourself more potential to lift heavy
  weights"*, while *"not rushing under early"* [E42.9].
- **Named drills** for the transition: 3-position snatches from the top down —
  mid-thigh, knee, floor; slow-pull snatches; snatch pull + snatch; halting
  snatch deadlifts to mid-thigh [E42.6, E32.4].
- **A pull drill with a dose:** *"always start from the power position and follow
  with the lowest hang position you're currently able to do well. Just a few sets
  of three to five total pulls each set should be plenty"* [E42.1]. The same
  source explains why pulls go wrong — athletes try to mimic the snatch or clean
  extension, and *"the motion shouldn't actually be identical"* [E42.1].

### The floating variation is the bridge

The hang position *"just off the floor"* — the floating snatch or clean — is
described as the one that trains the start position directly: it is
*"practically an entire snatch or clean, but what it adds is greater tension in
the starting position... This can help the lifter improve his or her starting
position by naturally forcing a position with the proper balance and posture,
which may not be easily found by the lifter with the bar on the floor. It will
also create more work for the back in particular"* [E9.12].

**For technical correction the order is pause > hang > blocks** — blocks are *"a
third and distant option... not really for technical adjustments"* [E9.2]. So
where a floor fault shows up, the first tool is a pause variation, then a hang
variation. This is unchanged from Block 1 and re-resolves to the same handle.

### The one instruction for overthinking it

Asked directly — hang snatch fine, floor snatch falls apart, too many things to
think about — the answer is: *"The best correction for over thinking is
practicing until you don't have to think"* [E9.6].

## Block Architecture

### Eight weeks: loading 9–14, back-off 15, test 16

- Amateurs typically run **8 or 12 week blocks**; taking four weeks off between
  blocks wastes *"25% of your total time, or in the extreme case 33% of your
  total training time"* [E1.12].
- Against over-lengthening: *"unnecessarily long cycles can actually slow their
  progress if heavy lifting and testing is limited. If an athlete at a current
  stage can PR the snatch and clean and jerk every few weeks, putting them into
  a training cycle that only gives them an opportunity to hit PRs every 12 weeks
  is not a great idea"* [E2.1].
- Everett's masters cycle is **6 weeks with no seventh week**, ending at a meet:
  *"There is no week 7 — this is the entire cycle"* [E5.11].

**A tension worth stating rather than smoothing.** A second source, working
through a snatch and clean & jerk progression numerically, sets the window
differently: *"we're doing something between probably at a minimum 12 weeks,
even the optimum is usually around 12 weeks, and the maximum we're going to go
for is about 16 weeks"* [E8.4]. That is a longer block than [E1.12]'s amateur
figure and longer than what [E2.1] recommends for a lifter who can still PR
often. The 8-week choice follows [E1.12] and [E2.1]; **it is a choice among
cited positions, not a consensus**, and [E8.4] is the position it declines.

Roll straight into Block 3 with no off-weeks [E1.12].

### Back-off week (15) — magnitude is genuinely disputed

The three positions re-resolve intact in today's pack, and they do not agree:

| Source | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Torokhtiy | Cut the weight **40–60%**, reduce 5×5 to 2–3×3–5, lengthen rest to 2–3 min, swap exercise variations [E4.3, E3.9]; sample week at **40–50% of 1RM**, 2–3 sets of 4–8 reps [E4.1, E3.1] |
| Catalyst back-off week (Mike Gray) | *"keep the load high but only decrease the reps"* [E3.2] |
| Greg Everett | reduce *"wts by 10-15% or so and volume by 15-25%"* [E5.11] |

**Prescription: Everett's version — weights −10–15%, volume −15–25%** [E5.11].
Chosen because it is the only one of the three given as a direct answer to what
recovery week follows a training cycle, and because Torokhtiy's own text says
beginners and lower-intensity trainees *"may require them less frequently"* than
advanced athletes lifting very heavy [E4.6, E3.11, E3.5]. **State this as
Everett's position, never as what "coaches say."** The choice among the three is
logged in the Judgment Ledger.

Note the Block 1 document rendered this as "volume −25%". Today's [E5.11] reads
"volume by 15-25%", and this document uses the range as written.

**Frequency is supported.** A general guideline is a deload after *"4-8 weeks of
intense training"* [E4.1, E3.1] — six loading weeks sits inside that — with the
caveat that it *"can vary from person to person"* [E3.5].

**After the back-off week, reintroduce load gradually:** *"Avoid jumping straight
back into your previous intensive levels of work... progressively increase sets,
reps and working weights over the course of the first week or even two"* [E3.5].
That instruction lands on week 16, which is a test week — see below.

### Test week (16)

- A newer lifter's max *"is by nature limited by technique, not
  strength/power/speed"*, and technique focus and lifting heavy *"are not
  mutually exclusive"* [E5.4, E37.8]. The same passage carries the harder line:
  *"Lots and lots of technique and NO missed lifts for the new lifter"* [E5.4].
- Stop the session at **form breakdown** — the most reliable indicator that
  further load produces diminishing returns and rising injury risk [E5.6].
- **What a heavy single actually means**, stated as a definition: *"taking the
  exercise to the heaviest weight for a single rep that can be managed in that
  training session. This is determined simply by gradually increasing the weight
  until that criterion is met without any failed attempts. If an attempt does
  fail, but the reason for failure is obviously technical in nature, the athlete
  can make another attempt. Otherwise the loading increase should stop when the
  athlete completes a rep he or she is confident is approximately the best
  possible at that time"* [E39.2].
- **Warm-up shape:** *"warm up and take bigger jumps earlier and smaller jumps as
  you approach your current 1RM"* [E39.3]. A concrete late-block ladder from a
  second source: singles at 80 / 85 / 90 / 95 / max [E16.3].
- **Do not build a complex warm-up.** For lifters in roughly their first two
  years, avoid hang snatches, snatch balances, presses and squats in the warm-up
  before maxing — it teaches over-pulling; *"we just want to practice that snatch
  in the warm-up room"*, with moderate jumps and plenty of sets at the lighter
  weights [E39.7].
- **An alternative to a true 1RM:** an AMRAP at roughly 90–95% *"will also give
  you a good idea of how well you've acclimatized to that increase in volume"*
  [E5.7].
- **Back squat 1RM frequency:** *"maybe one times per two months. I don't know no
  more"*, from a coach whose base work is *"like 4x3, like 5 by two"* [E16.9].

**Prescription:** test Snatch, Clean & Jerk, Front Squat and the snatch-balance
diagnostic [E10.3]. Back squat is tested only if it has not been maxed within
~2 months [E16.9]. Stop at the first form breakdown rather than the first miss
[E5.6, E39.2].

**Test-week lead-in.** Everett's pre-meet week runs *"Tuesday 85%, Wednesday 80%,
Thursday 70-75%, Friday off or very light (bar – 50% or so), and then compete
Saturday"*, with the caveat that *"what works best will vary among lifters"*
[E3.12]. Mapped onto Mon/Tue/Thu/Sat that becomes Mon 85% · Tue 80% · Thu
70–75% or very light · test Sat. **The mapping of a 5-day sequence onto this
athlete's 4-day week is [JUDGMENT — NO COVERAGE]** — the shape is Everett's, the
day assignment is not.

## Loading Framework

Everett's intensity taxonomy governs every prescription in this block [E25.3]:

| Purpose | Reps | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Technique | 1–3 | 50–70% |
| Power / speed | — | ~75% region |
| Hypertrophy | 5+ | 60–80%, closer to failure than usual at those intensities |
| True recovery | 2–3 | sub-60% |

Applied with the rep-end rule: **high end of the range for squats and basic
strength lifts, low end for snatch, clean and jerk**, to avoid *"unnecessary
disruption of optimal technical execution as a product of fatigue"* [E25.5].

Zone dosing supplies the set/rep grid [E25.4]:

- 70–80% → 3 reps × 4–5 sets ("the best tech practice, and the best training
  effect is achieved here")
- 80–90% → 1–3 reps × 2–4 sets
- 90%+ → 2–6 singles
- Strength lifts: *"3-5 sets of 3-5 reps for the squats, pulls, and presses"*,
  majority at RPE 8

Block-level allocation: *"do the majority of the lifts with loads between 70%
and 80-85%, the average amount with loads between 85% and 90%, and the minimal
number of reps with loads over 90%"*; even exceptional athletes stay under 13%
of work above 90% [E25.4].

**Prescribed weights over daily maxes**, subject to a stated precondition:
*"prescribing actual weights for your athletes is typically the best approach.
This is only possible when you have adequate experience with that athlete and a
history of training to refer to, but it allows you to know exactly what they've
done... rather than inadvertently going in circles"* [E16.7]. Seventeen months of
logged training plus a completed Block 1 satisfies that.

Where a daily max **is** used: stop at the rep max — *"once you hit your RM that
will be your last set for that exercise"* [E16.4]; keep mid-block singles
*"conservative... really crispy"* to build confidence [E16.10, E39.6]; and treat
miss-miss-make as a signal to reset [E16.6].

**Do not over-precision the loads.** By feel, a 5–10% error *"is normal, nothing
terrible"*; exact numbers matter only at genuine limit weights, where the
difference between 100 and 101 is the point [E27.10].

## Within-Block Progression — the gap Block 1 could not fill

Block 1 logged "within-block weekly load increments" as **[JUDGMENT — NO
COVERAGE]**, because q26 gave only a cross-cycle rule. Today's pack closes most
of that gap from a question Block 1 did not ask.

**The shape of a block, stated directly:** *"start with a lot of volume, then
over the course of a cycle I gradually bring the volume down, and then I start
with low intensity and over the course of that training block I bring the
intensity up"* [E36.3]. The same passage criticises a flat volume line —
*"at the moment your volume line is essentially staying the same throughout the
entire time"* — and gives the dwell time and the ladder: *"generally speaking
around two weeks with a certain rep range is good, so that 2, 4, 6 session kind
of bracket, move on then. So in this case if you've done 4×6 and 4×4 then I will
be going on to 6×3 or 6×2, then 4×2"*, adding *"do not be afraid to manipulate
the amount of sets to make up a small jump in intensity"* [E36.3].

**A second source describes the same arc for the classic lifts:** *"we're moving
from a sufficient amount of volume in that 70, 75, 80% range to give us
sufficient base to lift into the 80%, so we're moving through their kind of
5×3, 6×3, we're getting into our doubles, then we're moving into our singles and
then we're going for singles and then our max attempts"* [E36.11]. The same
source holds that total volume itself changes little across a career or across
block types, and that four to five sessions a week carries most lifters for
years [E36.11].

**What is still not covered: a per-week percentage increment.** The corpus gives
a rep-bracket ladder and a two-week dwell, not "+2.5% every Monday." The nearest
numeric rule remains cross-cycle: 2.5 kg steps applied to a **whole cycle on its
repetition** — threes 80 → 82.5, twos 85 → 87.5, ones 90 → 92.5 — with the value
located in *"the 20 sessions moving up to there"* rather than the final one
[E26.5, E36.7, E36.8]. **A fixed weekly percentage increment is
[JUDGMENT — NO COVERAGE]**; the block below progresses by rep bracket [E36.3],
not by a weekly increment.

**One exercise gets an explicit weekly-increase instruction**, and it is the
push press: load it, *"increase the weights each week if possible, but always
the limiting factor is that they need to be moving quickly. Don't worry too much
about what the weights are"* [E26.12].

**Timescale for judging whether any of this worked:** 12–16 weeks to see a
strength difference [E26.8, E41.9]. Block 2 ends at week 16 of the whole
program — the first point at which that window closes.

## Per-Decision Prescriptions

### The overhead — the Block 1 dosing fix, with a named protocol

Block 1's accepted carryover is that overhead-squat dosing was wrong. The
constraint has not changed:

- *"For weightlifters on a normal program starting off, they have no overhead
  squat volume programmed. That variation wouldn't really be something we
  typically program into any of our weightlifting blocks."* Where it is used, it
  is inside a snatch or snatch-balance complex, *"a frequency of about one or two
  times a week... total volume will probably be five, six, seven sets of kind of
  two or three reps, so it's never excessive"*, and even in a weak-overhead
  program *"it's not a standalone overhead squat"* [E10.8].
- The reason is opportunity cost: a 5×5 or 5×3 overhead-squat progression
  *"will really really sacrifice a lot of my recovery capital that I need for the
  snatch and for the jerk"* [E10.5].
- It is usually not a strength problem at all: *"the overhead squat is rarely an
  issue of strength. You usually have weak shoulders or poor mobility in the
  shoulders, neither of which need to be trained with an overhead squat"*
  [E10.5]; and it is *"natural to immediately assume there is insufficient
  strength"* when other elements are preventing the athlete from using strength
  they already have [E11.2].

**What today's pack adds that Block 1 did not have: an actual phased protocol,
with sets, reps and a duration.** Everett, answering a lifter whose overhead
squat lags the snatch — *"spend 2-3 weeks in each of the following phases,
working to add weight each week. Hold the bottom position of the last overhead
squat in each set for 3 seconds before recovering. Use 4-6 sets"* [E31.8]:

| Phase | Prescription |
|---|---|
| 1 | Snatch Push Press + Overhead Squat — 5+3 |
| 2 | Snatch Push Press + Overhead Squat — 3+2 |
| 3 | Overhead Squat — 3 · Snatch Push Press — 5 |
| 4 | Overhead Squat — 2 · Snatch Push Press — 3 |
| 5 | Overhead Squat — 1 |

Plus: *"any time you snatch, hold it in the bottom for 2-3 seconds, and add an
overhead squat at the end of each set. Warm-up at least 3 days/week with
overhead squats with an empty bar or up to maybe 50% of your best overhead squat
for a few triples"* [E31.8].

**Prescription: run phases 1–3 across weeks 9–14** (2 weeks each, 4–6 sets, 3-second
hold on the last rep of each set) [E31.8], inside the snatch complex rather than
as a standalone lift [E10.8]. Frequency 1–2×/week [E10.8]. The empty-bar/50%
overhead-squat warm-up runs on at least 3 training days [E31.8].

**This resolves Block 1's dosing error by substitution, not by argument**: the
Block 1 prescription of "5–7 sets of 2–3 with 5-second holds" [E10.8, E11.3] is
replaced by a source that gives a progression with a stated cadence. The 5-second
hold [E11.3] is retained for **snatch balances**, where [E11.3] actually places
it: *"practice this effort with overhead squats and snatch balances, both with
5 second holds in the bottom position before recovering."*

**Screen before loading behind-the-neck work.** *"Certain mobility limitations
will preclude the use of certain exercises. For example, limited shoulder and
thoracic mobility can mean some or all behind-the-neck lifts are inaccessible or
unsafe, even if they would otherwise be valuable for that athlete"* [E30.12].
Unchanged from Block 1; same handle.

**Position checklist, unchanged** [E11.11]: bar vertically over the back of the
neck, trunk leaned very slightly forward, head pushed forward through the arms;
shoulder blades fully retracted **and upwardly rotated**; elbows to end range.
*"If you can't create this stable base, you're going to have stability problems
no matter what else you do"* [E11.11]. And the inversion that keeps catching
people out: *"if you're leaning forward significantly with the bar overhead, I can
be pretty confident that you don't have a shoulder mobility restriction, because
such a position requires more mobility than a more upright one"* [E11.10].

**Depth helps stability:** *"If you're above full depth, you're allowing more
horizontal motion of the knees and hips, which is amplified in the arms
overhead"* [E11.12].

**Positional pulling for the upright posture:** halting snatch deadlifts or
snatch segment pulls with a pause at mid-thigh and/or the hip, and snatches
themselves paused at mid-thigh [E11.6].

### Powering more than you squat — the specific Block 2 failure mode

Moving to the floor after a hang block with power receiving positions creates
one predictable fault, and the corpus prescribes for it by name. The remedy list:
*"perform the full lifts only; add a squat to any lift you accidentally power;
hold all snatches in the bottom position for 3-5 seconds; add a front squat to
any clean or clean & jerk set (e.g. clean + front squat, or clean + front squat
+ jerk)"* [E31.7].

And for the clean specifically: *"I would do at least as many cleans in any given
period of time [as power cleans], and I would also squat after every power clean
(i.e. after receiving the power clean and stopping in that position, sit into a
squat). The only way you're going to fix the problem is to squat"* [E31.5].

**Prescription: clean + front squat as the standing complex through weeks 9–14**
[E31.7], and any accidental power receive is followed into the squat [E31.7,
E31.5].

**Why the full lift, not the power version, is the thing being trained:**
*"You won't get good at performing the full movements by training yourself to
power snatch and power clean. That's for sure"*, though power movements remain
useful *"only if they're used correctly in the program"* [E31.11]. A second
source adds the durability argument — athletes coached through a full-depth
catch *"are certainly a small bit more physically literate and they should be
able to control themselves slightly better"* [E31.1], and will *"almost certainly
be able to surpass their power clean or power snatch numbers"* [E31.6].

### The jerk — loading the split

Block 1 introduced the split jerk at empty bar. Block 2 loads it.

**The case for the split, from four passages:** it *"offers every possible great
advantage you can have for the jerk, which is why essentially everybody uses
it"*, while the power jerk's only advantage is simplicity and its margin for
error is *"very small"* [E14.1]. *"There's like 400 advantages of the split jerk
over the power jerk"* — broadest base in every direction, easiest to balance and
to correct a bar that drifts [E13.4]. *"There are lifters in the world who will
do better as power jerkers than as split jerkers, but they're the exception...
it takes time to determine if the power jerk is genuinely better for you or if
you just haven't learned how to split jerk well yet"* [E13.2, E13.5].

**Expect the fault, and know its cause.** *"It's very common for new lifters to
dip and drive forward when doing the split jerk"* [E13.2, E13.5]. The dip and
drive itself *"should be identical to that of the power jerk and even push
press — that is, it needs to be straight down, straight up and complete. The
lift only varies with the splitting of the feet"* [E14.5, E14.7].

**How it is built: repetitions, not exercise selection.** *"Push press from
split, jerk press from split are all mildly useful, but ultimately it comes down
to number of precise correct repetitions with the right split jerk positions"*
[E14.12]. And *"if you can't do it correctly and precisely without weight you
have no hope of doing it correctly and precisely with 100% of your clean and
jerk, so practice the correct position non-stop every day until you acclimatize,
until you accumulate hundreds and thousands of repetitions"* [E14.4]. **Daily
empty-bar split practice continues through Block 2** [E14.4] — same handle as
Block 1.

**Keep the power jerk; it is not a compromise.** *"If I train power jerk a lot,
if I do it like two or three times a week, it helps my split jerk so much... If I
ever have issues with the split jerk I'll do power jerks a couple times a week.
It normally fixes it right up"* [E14.3]. A second source separates the two
receiving positions and rates the power jerk for *"more leg strength, more
balance, better carryover to heavy jerks and split jerk"* than the push jerk
[E13.9].

**Balance rule:** *"Don't dive onto your front leg or reach your back foot so far
back that it pulls your hips out from under the bar"* [E14.8].

**Strength support** [E15.11]: the drive is *"primarily achieved by the quads"*,
so the front squat is the base lift; if squatting is adequate but the drive is
weak, use **partial front squats, jerk dip squats, jerk drives, jump squats**.
Trunk softening during the dip and drive absorbs the drive [E15.11].

**Pressing supports the jerk.** The "weak press, big jerk" boast is pushed back
on because those lifters are *"incredibly inconsistent"*: *"you can have a good
jerk without having a good press, but having a good press doesn't mean you're
gonna have a bad jerk, and it more than likely will make you have a better
jerk"* [E14.11, E13.12].

**Prescription:** split jerk from rack as the week's jerk primary, entering at
technique loading (1–3 reps @ 50–70%) [E25.3] and climbing into the 70–80% band
by weeks 13–14 [E25.4]; power jerk retained 2×/week [E14.3]; push press loaded
with speed as the limiter and weight increased weekly where speed holds [E26.12];
empty-bar split practice daily [E14.4]; front squat as the drive's base [E15.11].

### Squatting

The Block 1 correction stands and is not relitigated: **back squat primary,
roughly 2:1 back to front.**

- *"In general two back squats, one front squat for most of your career is going
  to be one of the best ratios you can do — it's going to be the most productive
  long-term"*; back squats are *"more useful"*, the front squat is *"something
  that you practice"*, and *"one good quality front squat a week is big"*
  [E18.3, E18.5]. The same source ranks the options and lands on
  one-front/two-back for *"a lot of your career"* [E18.11].
- *"Most of a weightlifter's lower body strength will be developed through back
  squats, especially in the beginning/intermediate years"*; the front squat *"is
  used more for strengthening the posture and positions of the clean"* [E18.4].
- *"Eliminating back squats will be a bad idea for 99% of you"* [E18.2].
- Back squats permit higher loads, put less stress on the knees, spare the
  shoulders and wrists, and are *"much more efficient with higher reps than front
  squats are. That makes them a perfect fit for base (strength) period"*
  [E18.10].
- Ratio norm: *"most lifters front squat about 85-90% of their back squat"*; below
  85% the fix is pause front squats, front-rack mobility, core and upper-back
  work [E18.7]. This athlete sits near 98% — the back squat is the lift with room
  in it.

**Rep band, for long limbs.** *"Most of us will be able to do a somewhat decent
squat with around 65 to 70% of our 1RM and it will look pretty good... but once I
start getting higher than that in terms of the weight, or once I start deviating
above three to five reps, I'll really start to notice those positions breaking
down"* [E20.10]. And load migrates to the back at **both** extremes — very high
reps and very heavy singles alike [E20.6]. The fix is twofold: live in the rep
range where technique survives, and *"find other exercises that let us attack
that weakness in their legs, primarily their quads"* [E20.6].

**Back-sparing quad work, named:** *"belt squats and single leg squats, because
those do an even better job of eliminating your low back from the movement"*,
with tempo emphasised for long limbs — *"really sit consciously slower, focus on
the pressure... keep it on your quads"* [E23.2]. Not universally liked: one
elite lifter reports never using a belt squat and finding it uncomfortable
[E23.5]. Recorded as a preference, not a refutation.

**Pause squats — both kinds, for different reasons.**
- Pause squats *"lack a really important function — training the ability to
  bounce out of the bottom that we need in heavy cleans... That magnified force
  is one of the reasons athletes collapse under cleans with weights well under
  what they can front squat"*, which is why bounce front squats stay in for a
  good portion of training [E19.6]. That ratio describes this athlete exactly.
- But pause squats are indicated *"if you're having a real issue with technical
  proficiency at the bottom of the squat — so if you squat down and you
  immediately shoot those knees back"* [E19.7] — the long-limb pattern.
- **Pause duration is CONTESTED.** Three seconds [E19.12] versus pausing only
  long enough to kill momentum, on the grounds that *"I have seen no convincing
  evidence that prolonged pause squats, so sitting in the bottom for two, three,
  five seconds, does anything at all. If anything, it just creates hip issues and
  knee issues"* [E19.1]. Both cited; no resolution offered.

**Prescription:** back squat 2× per week, front squat 1× [E18.3, E18.5, E18.11],
both in the 65–70% × 3–5 band as the working zone [E20.10], run at the high end
of the rep range [E25.5], progressing by rep bracket across the block [E36.3].
Bounce front squats retained alongside any pause work [E19.6]. Belt squat or
single-leg squat as the back-sparing quad builder [E23.2, E20.6].

**Back pain still points the other way, and that is still not resolved here.**
Front squat is preferred for a painful lower back *"because you keep a more
upright posture in the front squat"*, and a lifter with a bad lower back was
moved to *"the pulls from the blocks for a long time"* [E22.2]. The corpus points
one way on strength development and the other on back protection. Both are
cited; the trade-off is the athlete's.

**Squat frequency is an athlete override, not evidence.** The corpus holds both
*"one front one back"* per week [E22.2] and *"usually 4-6 days/week, sometimes
twice daily, and not necessarily with low volume"* [E20.11, E21.1]. The athlete
squats heavy ≥3×/week and tolerates it — revealed tolerance data the corpus
cannot contain. **That is an athlete override and is recorded as one**
(Judgment Ledger item 9), not as a cited prescription.

### Pulls

- Conventional band: **80–105%** of the best snatch or clean, which *"generally
  keeps pulls appropriately quick and technically sound rather than allowing them
  to get slow and change the lifter's balance and timing"* [E17.2, E17.3].
- **The exception that fits this athlete:** for *"newer lifters who nearly always
  have a surplus of strength relative to their technical ability to use that
  strength for the snatch and clean, this range of percentages is far too light"*
  [E17.9] — and the named profile is *"a lifter who has a poor overhead position
  in the snatch and consequently can't snatch as much as he or she would be
  expected to based on how much he or she can pull with ease"* [E17.4].
- Method where percentages fail: *"start conservatively and work your way up in
  fairly small increments until you reach a weight at which the athlete is moving
  properly and at the level of effort that you're looking for"* [E17.9].
- **Finish heavy pull series light and fast:** *"I also like to finish a series of
  very heavy pulls with a significantly lighter set to finish with speed rather
  than with the more grinding pulls. This last set or sets would be around 85-90%"*
  [E17.5].
- Snatch pulls specifically: *"maybe like 105 or 110 near the end if you feel
  comfortable with it and it's still moving fast and it looks the same
  technically"*; a pull that is *"abysmally slow"* is the fault signal, and speed
  dropping off in the second pull is acceptable *"as long as the positions don't
  change"* [E17.1, E42.2].
- **Pulls from the platform**, which is what Block 2 adds: *"No need to go beyond
  100% of your snatch or clean for this. Try to stick to the work in 90-100%
  intensity zone."* Hang or block pulls at 100–150% *"would better suit the base
  period"* [E17.8, E42.10].

**Prescription:** floor pulls in the 90–100% band [E17.8], hang/block pulls up to
100–150% in weeks 9–12 [E17.8], every heavy series finished with a light fast set
at 85–90% [E17.5], and the whole prescription overridden downward or upward by
what the bar actually looks like [E17.9].

### Trunk, accessories, mobility

- Trunk's job in the lifts is to *"act as a rigid support structure"*, so
  prioritize **static strength** [E24.1]. Aim it **anterior and lateral** — the
  classic lifts, squats and pulls already load the lower and middle back heavily,
  so that area *"will require somewhat less specific training"* [E24.3].
- **Placement:** stiff-legged deadlifts, RDLs and good mornings belong at the
  **end** of heavy sessions; lighter back extensions and reverse hyperextensions
  can run between heavy sessions and *"often prove recuperative"* [E24.1].
- Accessories: *"usually it's one or two bodybuilding [exercises] plus one core or
  one lower back"*, at the end of the session [E28.3, E40.10]. **Do not copy
  elite accessory lists** — those lifters are *"professional weightlifters with
  practically unlimited training time"* [E28.1].
- **Accessory dosing by time cap, not volume:** *"Most accessory work isn't going
  to cut too much into your recovery capacity... It's usually easier to give a
  time cap rather than a volume prescription, e.g. you've got 15 minutes to get
  this stuff done"* [E40.2].
- **When intensity rises, accessories taper — and this is the only passage in the
  pack that answers that question.** *"As you get close to a competition, cool it
  on the accessories. You don't necessarily have to cut them out completely, but
  at least go into maintenance phase and keep weights and reps the same for the
  last couple weeks, and then go lighter and less or none at all the final week.
  The effects of these exercises aren't going to vanish in a single week"*
  [E40.2].

  **Prescription: accessories to maintenance (same weights and reps) for weeks
  14–15, lighter or dropped in week 16** [E40.2].
- **Mobility dosing is CONTESTED, unchanged from Block 1.** Daily: *"if something's
  a problem you should be doing it pretty much every day until it's less of a
  problem"* [E30.8]; *"every day includes days outside the gym"* [E30.10].
  Not daily: *"it is not advised to exercise mobility every day because it can
  cause muscle soreness and fatigue"* [E30.1]. Recorded as contested.
- **Ankle protocol, if the diagnostic says ankles:** 15 kg minimum and realistically
  25 kg loaded onto the knee [E29.1], 3–5 sets of 90 seconds per side, daily,
  *"it took me about six months of weekly physical therapy and stretching my
  ankles every day"* [E29.8]. **Time-limited workaround** while it catches up:
  raise the heels and squat just above parallel to keep quad tension [E29.12] —
  explicitly *"not a long-term training strategy"* [E29.7].
- **The diagnostic itself** [E29.9]: empty-bar overhead squat at normal stance
  with a 5-second hold; repeat wider; repeat wider with toes out; then repeat the
  whole sequence with heels elevated on change plates. Noticeably easier with
  heels elevated → ankle mobility is limited; not noticeably better → the issue is
  hips, or both. Companion test: if overhead squats and heaving snatch balances go
  *"significantly more weight than you can snatch, the ankle isn't what's holding
  back your snatch"* [E29.3].
- **Test for whether mobility work is working at all:** *"if you don't see an
  improvement after one or two sessions, probably you're either doing it wrong or
  it's not going to work for what you want"* [E30.9].

### Autoregulation, misses, and technique under load

Block 2 raises the bar on the floor lifts. Three things follow.

**Technique does not improve on its own as the weight goes up.** *"No one's
[technique] really improves as you get heavier, at least"*, and the migration is
directional — *"if they have a staggered stance it gets more and more staggered,
if they have an elbow bend it gets more and more bent"* [E37.4].

**The Chinese rule is the cleanest autoregulation rule in the pack:** *"you're
not allowed go up unless it's correct. If there's even a tiny flaw it will be
corrected, it will be pointed out"* [E37.9]. A second passage from the same
interview describes the mechanism — a rep that is *"not as efficient"* means
*"he'll make you either do it again or go back down in the session"*, and a coach
who does not stop you *"is a green light"* [E37.3, E26.1].

**Prescription: correctness gates the next jump.** A rep that misses the block's
named position faults (early extension [E42.5], shoulders behind the bar too
soon [E32.1], accidental power receive [E31.7]) means repeat the weight or drop
back, not add [E37.9, E37.3].

**The crash fault, which is specific to loading the clean from the floor:**
*"the heavier weight decreases your confidence in your ability to get under the
bar in time. In response, you try to pull the bar higher... That attempt to pull
higher usually also means that you continue pulling longer — particularly leaning
back farther. This means it takes longer to get into the receiving position,
leaving the turnover slow and late, and contributing to the crashing effect. You
do have to pull a heavier bar harder, but because it's heavier, not because it
needs to go higher... the movement doesn't change, only the amount of force
you're applying"* [E37.11].

**After a miss — and the corpus disagrees with itself here.**

Everett's method: first decide whether it was a **mistake or a failure**. *"If
you miss a lift because you're not strong enough to make it, the decision should
be pretty obvious: stop. If you make a technical mistake and are confident you
can correct it, generally it's fine to repeat the set and make the correction. If
you miss again, consider the possibility that you didn't actually know what the
problem is"*; if tired, or a lot of session remains, or confidence has dropped —
move on [E38.1, E38.2]. And the extra attempts themselves cost: adding reps
*"you're adding to the stress of the program and creating effects that were"* not
planned [E38.1]. When resetting the weight: *"look back to the last set that was
done well, and that's a good place to start... if you're unsure, be conservative
and drop a bit more"* [E38.7].

Against it, plainly: *"a lot of coaches say you should never miss in training,
misses in training should be very very rare — okay, that also means like you're
taking lifts that you should never miss. Misses are a part of weightlifting, you
can't avoid that, and if you train to not miss that's a horrible mentality"*
[E38.8].

**Prescription: Everett's mistake/failure test** [E38.1, E38.2, E38.7], **stated
as Everett's position.** The dissent [E38.8] is real and comes from a competing
elite lifter; it is not folded into a consensus. The choice between them is
logged in the Judgment Ledger.

**RPE is a weak instrument for a self-coached athlete.** *"In practice, RPE and
RIR are arguably just as variable as percentages. The real difference is that
we're shifting the choice of weight from the coach to the athlete... lifters who
tend to always push as hard as possible are going to underestimate their effort,
which results in higher intensity"* [E27.7, E35.4, E35.8]. The available
mitigation is the same source's: write down the predicted RPE on top sets and
check it against what happened [E27.7, E35.4]. Warm-up feel does not predict the
session — *"you can warm up really really good but then you might not make the
lift, but you can warm up really really bad then make the lift"* [E27.2, E39.1].

**A named tool for the exact Block 2 failure mode.** *"If technically you're
perfect but you break down hard at heavier weights, tempo snatch is what you
need. It's going to teach you how to hold position when the bar is not moving as
fast as you're used to it moving"* [E12.2]. This passage was returned by the
muscle-snatch question, which it does not answer — see the retrieval notes below.

## Day-by-Day Template

**Four days: Mon · Tue · Thu · Sat.** The week itself is cited: *"pretty much
everyone training today should be training four times a week ideally... usually
you'll do something like Monday Tuesday Thursday Saturday. This means at the
start of the week when you're quite fresh get a lot of valuable work done, then
you have a rest day in between each of those last sessions and you get a full lay
off on Sunday"* [E8.12]. And *"never more than two days in a row"* [E8.9]. Three
sessions a week is where meaningful long-term progress starts and four is *"the
sweet spot"* [E8.6, E8.3].

**Which session lands on which calendar day is [JUDGMENT — NO COVERAGE].** The
Mon/Tue/Thu/Sat *frame* is cited [E8.12]; the assignment of the heaviest snatch
work to Monday and the reduced session to Thursday is driven entirely by the
night-shift sleep pattern, which the corpus does not address. Note that this
mapping **differs from Block 1's school-term labels**, which put the leg session
on Wednesday; Wednesday is not a training day in Block 2.

**Two rules govern every session:**
- **Snatch first.** *"If there's snatching in the session, put it first, put it
  before the clean and jerk, put it before the squat"* [E7.12]. The caveat is
  kept: if a few light snatch sets measurably wreck the squats, the problem is
  general conditioning, not the ordering [E7.12].
- **Touch both lifts every session.** *"The only real practice for the snatch and
  clean and jerk is the snatch and clean and jerk... better to do both, some form
  of those exercises every single session"* — power snatch, snatch balance, power
  clean all count [E8.1].

Sessions run to about **90 minutes**, longer only for more experienced athletes
with more recovery capacity [E6.5]. **How many exercises fit in that is
[JUDGMENT — NO COVERAGE]** — q6 returns six passages and none of them answers the
question; the widely repeated "4–6 exercises per session" rule has no support
anywhere in this pack.
